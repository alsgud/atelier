How the dashboard works, before it gets drawn

You picked the look: the window and its light, North light at night, the sidebar, dark by default. Before any more screens, this page walks through how the whole dashboard behaves: who it’s for, where everything lives, how each journey goes, and how every screen is laid out. It ends with numbered decisions. Read it top to bottom and answer them in one reply.

Design task D5 · 26 Oct 2026 · low-fi on purpose: structure first, high-fi after your answers

In short

  • The Inbox opens first, everywhere: the top decision shown large, the rest waiting beside it.
  • Two levels only: things across all projects, then one page per project and one per task.
  • A status is a dot and one word from a list of four; red only when you are needed.
  • Money and plan usage sit quietly in the sidebar and the phone’s top bar, not on every page.
  • Decisions happen at once with Undo; only the irreversible asks you to confirm.

About 25 minutes to read. Every decision has a recommendation, so “all as recommended” is a full answer.

1

Who it’s for, and what the best tools already do

You run a team of AI agents across several projects the way a senior manager runs a team: you set direction, read plain summaries, make the few calls only you can make, and rarely debug. You look in for a minute between other things, at your desk and on your phone. The dashboard is worth it only if managing the team costs you less time than doing the work yourself.

The jobs you hire the dashboard for

  • J1
    Check in and decide. Does anything need me? If so, see it and decide in one or two taps. Most visits are this.
  • J2
    See what the team is doing. Who is working on what, is anyone stuck, is the work real.
  • J3
    Start a project. Name it, give the lead a brief, choose how much you want to approve, go.
  • J4
    Watch a task and its proof. What is it doing now, how far along, and show me it works.
  • J5
    Control spend. What did today cost, how close to the limit, how much of my own Claude plan is left.
  • J6
    Review a design or a milestone. Look at the thing itself, approve in my words or send it back.
  • J7
    Recover when something fails. What broke, what it costs me, the one thing to do about it.
  • J8
    Correct the lead. Answer, overrule, write a ruling, restart it when it drifts.

References board

What the research says

  1. One queue, triaged by urgency, that you can empty.

    Linear’s inbox splits “Priority” from “Other”; GitHub and Superhuman sell zero as the goal. Filters and rule builders (GitHub’s notification filters, Linear’s triage rules) are for teams with thousands of items; you have tens.

    So: one Inbox, two groups (Needs you now · When you have a minute), no filters, a calm zero. J1

  2. Status is a dot and a word, from a short fixed list.

    Vercel uses five (Ready, Error, Building, Queued, Canceled), GitHub four. Nobody explains a status in a paragraph.

    So: four meanings everywhere: Working (lilac), Needs you (red), Done (green), and neutral for Waiting, Stopped, Off. Each task, desk and project uses the same words. J2 J4

  3. Progress is a shape you can see, not a counter.

    GitHub’s run graph shows jobs as boxes with durations; Devin’s progress tab lists steps with a replay timeline. “Iteration 0 of 6” says nothing (your pain point 3).

    So: a task shows its stage (Planning, Building, Checking, Review, Done), the latest step in a sentence, and “last active 2 min ago”. UX-5 J4

  4. Failures open at the failure, everything else folded.

    GitHub Actions opens the failed step’s log and folds the green ones.

    So: a failed task opens on what went wrong, in one sentence, with what the lead is doing about it; the log tail sits under Details. J7

  5. Approving shows the thing, and asks for words only when you say no.

    GitHub’s PR review puts the state beside the title and the verdict in one place; a comment is optional to approve and expected to request changes.

    So: the Inbox item shows the design, proof or question itself; rejecting asks for a note, approving a gate asks for a few words (as today, they go to the designer). J6

  6. Confirm only what can’t be undone; undo the rest.

    Vercel’s redeploy dialog names exactly what will happen and where. Linear never confirms a triage action: it offers undo.

    So: decisions are instant with Undo in the toast; stopping a lead, cancelling a task and running an audit (it spends money) ask first, in one sentence. UX-10 J1 J7

  7. The phone is a companion, not the desktop squeezed.

    Linear calls its app “the portable companion”: inbox, quick actions, swipes. GitHub Mobile shows agent sessions as a list with a status line.

    So: on the phone, Inbox, Office, project Now and the task page are first-class; Settings and Docs work but are laid out for the desk. UX-2 J1 J2

  8. Agents are teammates with a place, not log streams.

    Linear shows an agent as an assignee in the issue’s properties, with its updates in the activity. Devin’s session is a page you can come back to.

    So: every agent has a desk in the Office and every task has a page; you never read raw logs unless you open Details. UX-8 J2

  9. History is a list: status, one line, time.

    Zapier’s Zap history, n8n executions and Vercel deployments all read the same way, filterable by status.

    So: Activity is one feed by day with a project filter and “Only problems”. UX-13 J2 J7

  10. Keyboard first on the desk.

    Raycast and Linear: one palette (⌘K) to go anywhere or do anything, single keys for the common moves.

    So: ⌘K to jump to any project, task or setting; J/K, Enter, Z (undo), G then I/O/P/A to move. J1

What to avoid, all together: filter builders, avatars on every row, IDs and branch names in the first line, three-pane log views, status pills in five colours, marketing gradients and glass, and settings pages that are walls of toggles.

Layout system

No twelve-column grid. Every screen is one fluid column with a reading limit, plus at most one fixed column beside it: the Inbox queue, or a task’s facts. Three widths only (desk, middle, phone), so each screen has one plan per width and nothing in between to guess. Why: dashboards with many columns ask you to scan; this one asks you to read one thing and decide.

Density

Compact on the desk, roomy under the thumb. Desk rows are Linear-dense (36 px, 14 px text) because you scan them with a mouse and keys; reading text inside an item is 16 px on a 24 px line, because that is where you decide. On the phone every row and control is at least 44 px, and the meta line moves under the title instead of beside it. Spacing is the 4 px scale in tokens.css; nothing uses a value outside it.

Five kinds of screen

List and detail, or a full page

Side by side: only the Inbox

You go through the Inbox in order, one decision after another, so the list stays beside the open item (direction B, ruling R-1). Choosing a row changes the item; the list keeps its scroll. On the phone the item fills the screen and the list follows it.

A page of its own: everything else

A project or a task is something you read, not a queue you work through, so it opens as a page with a back link and its own address. Whether a task can also open as a peek beside the Office or a task list is UX-4.

Where the dock and the toasts live

The decision dock

  • Desk: stuck to the foot of the reading column, the same place for every item, 20 from the panel’s edge. The item scrolls under it.
  • Phone: 8 above the tab bar, buttons full width and 44 tall; the note line grows up to 160 then scrolls.
  • Task page: the same dock, only while the task needs you (a question, a plan to approve). Otherwise there is none: no dock means nothing to decide.
  • Never two docks on one screen; never a dock on a page that only shows things.

Toasts

  • With a dock on screen: the toast rises out of the dock and covers only the dock, as wide as it, never a tab or another button (measured in D3).
  • Without a dock: bottom centre of the content column (not over the sidebar); on the phone 8 above the tab bar.
  • One at a time; a new one replaces the old. 6 s, paused while hovered or focused; Undo or Try again is its only button.

Information design: one way to show each thing

Six things appear all over the dashboard: a project, a task, a decision, proof, money and progress. Each has one look, used on every screen, so you learn it once. Every number comes from the one summary the server sends (R2): the Inbox count, the sidebar, the Office and the project page show the same figure because they read the same field, never because each screen adds it up.

A project

replayBuilding · 4 of 9 outcomes met · 1 needs you
  • Name as you typed it, then one sentence: phase, outcomes met, what needs you. Lead dot: lilac when on, hollow when off.
  • The same row in Projects, the sidebar (name + waiting count) and the Office floor’s heading.
  • The GitHub repo shows only when something is wrong with it.

A task

Trim clips on the timelinereplay · Building · last active 1 min ago
  • What it delivers, in your words (its title), never its ID or branch. Then project · stage · last active.
  • Status is a dot and one word from four: Working, Needs you, Done, and neutral for Waiting, Stopped, Off.
  • ID, branch, files and PR live under Details on its page.

A decision

replay · Budget replay has used today’s $40, so new work is paused Raise today to $60Leave it paused
  • Eyebrow: project · kind. Title: what is being asked, as a sentence. Body: the thing itself. Dock: verbs that say what happens.
  • Buttons are named for their result (“Raise today to $60”), never OK / Cancel / Submit.
  • Two groups only: Needs you now (red count) and When you have a minute (neutral).

Proof

Shown working · 14:02 · recording 0:48
  • Pictures first, large (16:10, captioned with what they show), then the recording with its length, then the run log’s last lines under Details.
  • Always served from the view port; opens in a new tab to click around.
  • No proof yet says so: “Proof appears when the task shows its work.”

Money

replay today$40.00 of $40
Paused until you raise it or midnight
  • Dollars with cents, tabular figures, “of” the limit when there is one. A meter only against a limit; without one, just the figure.
  • Red only at the limit, because only then it needs you. “API-equivalent, not a bill” is said once, in the spend breakdown.
  • One spend figure per screen (UX-9); the per-task split opens under it, from the summary.

Progress

PlanningBuildingCheckingReviewDone Wiring the trim handles into the timeline
  • A task: its stage, the latest step in a sentence, last active (UX-5). A project: the phase track (Brief · Spec · Design · Contracts · Build) and outcomes met.
  • Quiet for longer than usual is neutral text (“Quiet for 25 min, longer than usual”), never red; it becomes red only when the lead says it’s stuck.

Time

Under an hour: “1 min ago”, “40 min ago”. Today: “14:02”. This week: “Mon 14:02”. Older: “12 Oct”. The exact time is always in the tooltip and to screen readers. Durations: “48 s”, “12 min”, “1 h 20 min”.

Empty, loading, error and stale

Every screen has all four, written down so frontend never invents one. Four rules first, then each screen.

  1. Loading shows the shape, once. A skeleton of the real layout on the first load of a screen only; counts are hidden until they are known. Refreshes never show a skeleton again (R7).
  2. Empty says what will be here, and the one thing to do. One sentence, at most one button. Never an illustration, never “No data”.
  3. An error says what happened and what happens next, in one line, inside the page; the sidebar and tabs stay. Neutral when it fixes itself (it retries), red only when you have to act.
  4. Stale is a time, not a warning. “Last updated 08:12 · Retrying” in the page’s head; decide buttons are disabled with the reason while the server can’t be reached.

A confirm is one short dialog: a question naming the thing, what happens, and two buttons named for their result. The safe one has the focus; Esc is the safe one. Never a checkbox to type “I understand”.

Live refresh (R7)

  • Every 15 s and when you come back to the tab, as today. Changes apply in place: no reload, no jump.
  • What you are reading stays. Your scroll, open folds and the text you are typing are never touched. The open Inbox item is never swapped for a newer one; a new urgent item appears in the queue, in its place, and the count changes.
  • Decided somewhere else (your phone, while the desk is open): the item says “You decided this on another device” and leaves when you move on.
  • Lists never reorder under your cursor or finger. A row that should move waits until the pointer leaves the list.

Notifications UX-15

  • Inside the app: the Inbox count in the sidebar and tab bar (red only for “Needs you now”, neutral otherwise), the tab title “(2) Atelier”, and a dot on the browser tab’s icon.
  • Toasts only answer something you did; the dashboard never toasts on its own.
  • Outside the app: nothing today; push for “Needs you now” is UX-15.
